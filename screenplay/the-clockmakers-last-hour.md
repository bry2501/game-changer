## Evidence 0: The household log (the prior)

*On the side table in the study. The player searches it; the evidence screen opens.*

**Evidence card: the household log, tonight (6 – 10 pm)**

| Name | Signed in | Signed out |
|---|---|---|
| Poe | — | — |
| Clara | 7:00 pm | 10:00 pm |
| Mr. Thomas | 4:00 pm | 8:00 pm |
| Edgar | — | — |

> **GHOST** *(thought)*: This was my own rule, and I remember the day I made it: everyone signs the household log when they come and go, the vicar included. A house should know who is in it. It shows who was inside tonight between 6 pm and 10 pm, and for how long. Poe had asked for the evening off to visit his sister; I remember saying yes. That is why I made my own tea. Edgar went home at six, as he always does. That leaves two people who were in this house with my tea: Clara and Mr. Thomas. The question is, which of the two?
>
> **GHOST** *(thought)*: Before I examine a single clue, I need a starting point to stand on. If I had no information to go on, each suspect would begin with an equal 50/50 chance of guilt. But I can use the log to count the hours each of them spent under this roof: the longer someone was here, the more chances they had. That starting belief is called the PRIOR. It is a judgment, not a fact.

**Clara** · *How long was Clara inside?* The log says Clara signed in at 7:00 pm and out at 10:00 pm. How many hours between 6 and 10 pm was she in the house? *(four blocks, “1 hour” to “4 hours”; choosing one lights it and every block to its left; the player lights the right number to continue)*
If wrong: “Not quite. Check the times again: in at 7:00 pm, out at 10:00 pm, and only the hours between 6 and 10 pm count.”
Once right: *Clara was inside for 3 hours.* The lit blocks are Clara’s share of all the time anyone spent inside, 3 out of 5 hours. More time inside means more chances to reach my tea, so a bigger starting share of suspicion.

**Mr. Thomas** · *How long was Mr. Thomas inside?* The log says Mr. Thomas signed in at 4:00 pm and out at 8:00 pm. How many hours between 6 and 10 pm was he in the house? *(four blocks, “1 hour” to “4 hours”; choosing one lights it and every block to its left; the player lights the right number to continue)*
If wrong: “Not quite. Check the times again: in at 4:00 pm, out at 8:00 pm, and only the hours between 6 and 10 pm count.”
Once right: *Mr. Thomas was inside for 2 hours.* The lit blocks are Mr. Thomas’s share of all the time anyone spent inside, 2 out of 5 hours.

**Where my suspicion starts**: Each person’s share of the time inside becomes their starting share of suspicion: whoever spent longest in the house starts highest. That’s my starting point. *(the hour blocks from the two log pages slide into one row of five, the gaps close, and the row becomes the starting bar; each name sits under its share with “N of 5 hours”)**

> **Designer’s note (not shown in game):** prior = hours inside ÷ total: Clara 3/5 = 60%, Mr. Thomas 2/5 = 40%. Clara starts ahead purely on opportunity.
>
> - The prior is a *subjective* starting point (degree of belief), and hours-inside is a transparent stand-in for it. Any coherent starting point is legitimate; what matters is that the evidence is allowed to move it.
> - The log is also what closes the hypothesis set. The stretch step in every update is only valid because “Clara” and “Mr. Thomas” are mutually exclusive and cover every possibility. The ghost says so out loud; the ending restates it. If you ever add a third suspect, give them a share.
> - Neither share is ever 0% or 100%, and no rating maps to 0 or 1, so no belief can become immune to later evidence.
> - The hours-inside rule counts time before the tea existed. That is acceptable only because the ghost does not yet know when the tea was tampered with. A stricter alternative would count only the half hour the cup stood on the desk.

> **GHOST** *(thought)*: Clara was here the longest, so she starts as the likeliest. But that doesn’t mean she’s guilty yet.
> **GHOST** *(thought)*: Every piece of evidence from now on will shift these odds. By how much is the whole question.
> **GHOST** *(thought)*: Clara’s room is upstairs. I know my niece. If she wrote something, the draft will be in her writing desk.

## Clue 1 of 7: An Angry Letter

*Clara’s Room (upstairs): the player searches the writing desk. The drawer sticks, then slides out with a small wooden cry; a chime; the evidence screen opens.*

### Page: The clue

*I found this letter at the back of Clara’s writing-desk drawer, folded small, then smaller. It is a draft, crossed out and rewritten, the nib pressed so hard on the last line that it nearly tore the paper.*

**Evidence card: An Angry Letter**

```
                                                    Tuesday
Uncle —
    If you change your will, I will NEVER forgive you!
                                                    C.
```

> **GHOST** *(thought)*: Tuesday. Three days ago: the day we quarrelled about my will. A letter in a drawer is a letter nobody read.
> **GHOST** *(thought)*: It shows strong feelings, maybe even a motive. But someone really was poisoned tonight, so the question is not whether anger leads to murder. It is whether this letter fits one story better than the other.

### Page: Lesson 1: Think in stories

Don’t ask “who does this evidence point to?” Instead, go one person at a time and imagine they are the murderer. Then ask: assuming they are the murderer, how likely is it that I would find this evidence? Imagine a hundred different possible worlds in which that person is the murderer: on how many of them would this clue turn up? Each suspect is a story, and a clue fits some stories better than others.

*(Below: the current suspicion bar, names only.)*

### Page: Suppose Clara is the murderer.

**How likely would I be to find an angry letter like this?**

> **GHOST** *(thought)*: If Clara is the murderer, the quarrel over my will would be her reason, and a furious letter is exactly the footprint that kind of anger leaves. But Clara writes furious letters all the time, and has never hurt a soul. So her guilt makes this letter more expected, though not by a huge margin.

[ 1 Very unlikely 10% ] [ 2 Unlikely 30% ] [ 3 As likely as not 50% ] [ 4 Likely 70% ] [ 5 Very likely 90% ]

After choosing: “You rated it N: …, a P% chance.”

### Page: Suppose Mr. Thomas is the murderer.

**How likely would I be to find an angry letter like this?**

> **GHOST** *(thought)*: If Thomas is the murderer, nothing about that Tuesday changes. Clara and I still quarrelled, she still went upstairs and wrote the most savage thing she could think of, and she still didn’t send it to me. His guilt does not stop this letter existing, so it is only a little less expected here than in her story.

[ 1 Very unlikely 10% ] [ 2 Unlikely 30% ] [ 3 As likely as not 50% ] [ 4 Likely 70% ] [ 5 Very likely 90% ]

After choosing: “You rated it N: …, a P% chance.”

### Page: Update (the player steps through, on a square of possible worlds)

- **Before**: Each person’s column is as wide as their share of suspicion. *(Key: Width of a column: that person’s share of suspicion.)*
- **Step 1 of 3: shrink**: Each bar shrinks by how well the clue fits that person’s story. A good fit keeps most of it; a poor fit keeps little. The shaded part is what is left; the faded part is what the clue took away. *(Key: Side brackets: the evidence. Each shaded piece is the part of that person’s story in which this clue turns up; your rating sets its height.)*
- **Step 2 of 3: together**: Put what’s left side by side. It no longer fills the whole square, because the clue took a piece of every share. The less that’s left, the more unusual this clue was across both stories; the more, the more ordinary. *(Key: Shaded pieces: the worlds that fit this clue. Everything else is ruled out.)*
- **Step 3 of 3: stretch**: Stretch what’s left back to the full square, keeping the proportions. Whoever kept more than the others grows; whoever kept less shrinks. This is only fair because these two stories cover every possibility between them. *(Key: New widths: the new shares. Dashed line: where the old border was.)*
- Step 1 labels each suspect “rated N: keeps very little / keeps less than half / keeps half / keeps most / keeps almost all”; step 3 marks each ▲ grew / ▼ shrank, with the old border dashed, and says in words how each share changed.

### Page: The math

*(The same update as Bayes’ rule, one hypothesis per suspect: H1 “Clara is the murderer”, H2 “Mr. Thomas is the murderer”, and E finding an angry letter like this. P(H1 | E) = P(H1) · P(E | H1) ÷ [P(H1) · P(E | H1) + P(H2) · P(E | H2)]. One formula that transforms in place as the player steps through: the rule in symbols with a key; then each symbol becomes the person or the clue (P(Clara) · P(letter given Clara) over the same for both suspects); then each becomes the player’s number, worked to the answer, with the second suspect’s line beneath.)*

- **P(H1), P(H2)**, your board so far: Clara 60%, Mr. Thomas 40% *(numbers here follow the designed ratings)*
- **P(E | H1), P(E | H2)**, your ratings: Clara 70%, Mr. Thomas 50%
- **The denominator**, the clue at all: 60% × 70% + 40% × 50% = 42% + 20% = 62%
- **P(H1 | E), P(H2 | E)**, the new board: Clara 68%, Mr. Thomas 32%

### Page: Recap: Vivid but weak evidence

An angry letter feels like a confession. But people write angry letters all the time and hurt no one. A clue only shifts suspicion as much as it fits one story better than the others. Notice how little the board moved: if your two ratings were far apart, it swung much further than a never-sent draft deserves.

*(Below: your board before this clue, and your board now, as two squares.)*

> **Designer’s note (not shown in game): the intended reasoning**
>
> - Intended ratings (1–5 map to 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9): Clara **4** (0.7), Mr. Thomas **3** (0.5). Likelihood ratio 1.4 : 1 for Clara, barely worth mentioning.
> - With those ratings: before Clara 60%, Mr. Thomas 40% → keep 60% × 0.7 = 42%; 40% × 0.5 = 20% → together 62% → stretched: **Clara 68%**, **Mr. Thomas 32%**.
> - Trap: *Vivid but weak evidence*. A player who rates 5 / 1 jumps to about 93% Clara on a draft that was never sent.

*The overlay closes. Back in the house.*

> **GHOST** *(thought)*: Clara. She was so angry with me...
> **GHOST** *(thought)*: Anger makes a vivid story. But a vivid story isn’t the same as a likely one.
> **GHOST** *(thought)*: Clara’s letter was about my will. Let me see what I actually wrote. The draft should be in my desk drawer.

- **Step 2 of 3: together**: Put what’s left side by side. It no longer fills the whole square, because the clue took a piece of every share. ## Appendix: the intended reasoning chain (designer’s notes)

**How ratings become numbers.** A rating is the likelihood P(clue | this person is the murderer): 1 → 0.1, 2 → 0.3, 3 → 0.5, 4 → 0.7, 5 → 0.9. It never reaches 0 or 1, so no story can be ruled out, or proved, by one clue. Only the *ratio* of the two ratings moves the board, which is why a clue rated the same for both suspects changes nothing.

The board after each clue, if the player’s ratings match the intended ratings (a model answer, not an answer key):

| After | Ratings (Clara / Mr. Thomas) | Likelihood ratio | Clara | Mr. Thomas |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Start (household log) | — | — | 60% | 40% |
| 1. An Angry Letter | 4 / 3 | 1.4 : 1 for Clara (weak) | 68% | 32% |
| 2. The Draft Will | 5 / 5 | 1 : 1 (old news) | 68% | 32% |
| 3. A Muddy Boot Print | 1 / 3 | 5 : 1 for Thomas (strong) | 30% | 70% |
| 4. The Foxgloves | 2 / 5 | 3 : 1 for Thomas (moderate) | 12% | 88% |
| 5. The Chemist’s Slip | 4 / 2 | 2.33 : 1 for Clara (moderate) | 25% | 75% |
| 6. The Telephone Log | 1 / 3 | 5 : 1 for Thomas (strong) | 6% | 94% |
| 7. A Pruning Knife | 3 / 4 | 1.4 : 1 for Thomas (weak) | 4% | 96% |

**Arc.** Clara leads, then loses ground to the print and the foxgloves, then comes back with the slip just when the player thinks it is settled, then is cleared by the alibi. The two clues that move the board least (the will, the knife) teach the lessons about old news and about clues that fit both stories. The twist and the alibi are what make the ending earn its relief.

**Timeline.** The investigation takes place between ten and eleven. Clara is in the kitchen for the whole hour and comes to the study at eleven, as she does every night. Tea at 9:30; drunk a little before ten; Clara’s call 9:28 to 9:57; Thomas signed out at eight; the chemist at 6:40; Clara signed in at seven; the quarrel and the letter on Tuesday; the signing on Monday at ten. Edgar mops the conservatory floor before he leaves at six, so any print there after six is from tonight.

**Checks run** (by the generator, every time). Every posterior above is computed from the ratings. The final board is the same for all 5,040 orderings of the seven clues. Starting Clara at 10%, 50%, 90% and 99% (same ratings) ends at 0.3%, 3.0%, 22% and 75%: priors wash out only when everyone shares the same likelihoods. Player-error cases, for the feedback text: rating the letter 5 / 1 ends at 23% Clara; rating the will 5 / 2 (counting the quarrel twice) ends at 12%; dismissing the slip (3 / 3) ends at 2.0%; over-reacting to it (5 / 1) ends at 15%.

**Assumptions the game makes.**

1. One culprit, and the two suspects cover every possibility, so the stretch step is valid.
2. Each clue’s likelihood depends only on who the murderer is, not on other clues found (conditional independence given the culprit). The letter and the will concern the same quarrel, so the will is deliberately rated as old news rather than as new motive evidence.
3. Background facts (Thomas leaves his bicycle by the garden gate; Poe and Edgar were out; Clara nags about John’s sleep; the single Monday signing threatens Clara and Thomas equally) are established before a clue uses them.
4. Verbal labels map to fixed numbers for every player. People read “likely” differently, so the labels and numbers must stay locked together.
5. The ghost searches where he already suspects, so clues are not a random sample. Likelihoods should be judged as “would I have found this here whoever the murderer is?” The foxglove reaction has the ghost admit he has not looked for evidence against Thomas, and the slip is found because he then does.
6. The ghost’s unmodelled facts (the 7:48 call, what was really in the bottle) stay unexplained on purpose. The verdict does not need them.
