## Evidence 0: The household log (the prior)

*On the side table in the study. The player searches it; the evidence screen opens.*

**Evidence card: the household log, tonight (6 – 10 pm)**

| Name | Signed in | Signed out | Inside, 6–10 pm |
|---|---|---|---|
| Graves | — | not in tonight (evening off) | — |
| Clara | 7:00 pm | 10:00 pm (still in) | 3 h |
| Mr. Voss | 4:00 pm (before the window) | 8:00 pm | 2 h |
| Pip | — | out 6:00 pm (went home) | — |

> **GHOST** *(thought)*: This was my own rule, and I remember the day I made it: everyone signs the household log when they come and go, the vicar included. A house should know who is in it. It shows who was inside tonight between 6 pm and 10 pm, and for how long. Graves had asked for the evening off to visit his sister; I remember saying yes. That is why I made my own tea. Pip went home at six, as he always does. That leaves two people who were in this house with my tea: Clara and Mr. Voss. Everything I weigh from here on is a contest between those two. If I have overlooked a third, none of it will be worth much. If you had asked me this morning whether Clara or Edmund would poison my tea, I would have laughed at either. But someone did, so the question is no longer whether such a person exists. It is which of the two.
>
> **GHOST** *(thought)*: Before I weigh a single clue, I need somewhere to stand. I do not yet know when anyone could have reached my tea, so I will count the hours each of them spent under this roof: the longer someone was here, the more chances they had. That starting belief is called the PRIOR. It is a judgment, not a fact. In total, the log shows 5 hours’ worth of visitors in my mansion.

**Clara** · *Clara was inside for 3 hours.* (shown as half-hour blocks, Clara’s lit).
The lit blocks are Clara’s share of all the time anyone spent inside. More time inside means more chances to reach my tea, so a bigger starting share of suspicion.

**Mr. Voss** · *Mr. Voss was inside for 2 hours.* (shown as half-hour blocks, Mr. Voss’s lit).
The lit blocks are Mr. Voss’s share of all the time anyone spent inside.

**Where my suspicion starts**: Each person’s share of the time inside becomes their starting share of suspicion. *(a single bar, names only)*

**What the starting point means**: Before finding any clues, suspicion starts where the opportunity was: whoever spent longest in the house starts highest. That’s my starting point. Every clue from here on out will update and shift it.

> **Designer’s note (not shown in game):** prior = hours inside ÷ total: Clara 3/5 = 60%, Mr. Voss 2/5 = 40%. Clara starts ahead purely on opportunity.
>
> - The prior is a *subjective* starting point (degree of belief), and hours-inside is a transparent stand-in for it. Any coherent starting point is legitimate; what matters is that the evidence is allowed to move it.
> - The log is also what closes the hypothesis set. The stretch step in every update is only valid because “Clara” and “Mr. Voss” are mutually exclusive and cover every possibility. The ghost says so out loud; the ending restates it. If you ever add a third suspect, give them a share.
> - Neither share is ever 0% or 100%, and no rating maps to 0 or 1, so no belief can become immune to later evidence.
> - The hours-inside rule counts time before the tea existed. That is acceptable only because the ghost does not yet know when the tea was tampered with. A stricter alternative would count only the half hour the cup stood on the desk.

> **GHOST** *(thought)*: Clara was here the longest, so she starts as the likeliest. But that doesn’t mean guiltiest yet.
> **GHOST** *(thought)*: Every piece of evidence from now on should shift these shares. By how much is the whole question.
> **GHOST** *(thought)*: Clara’s room is upstairs. I know my niece. When she is angry, Clara writes. She always has: pages of it, scorching, and then she never sends them. If she wrote to me after our quarrel, the draft will be in her writing desk.

> **GHOST** *(thought)*: Tuesday. Three days ago: the day we quarrelled about my will. A letter in a drawer is a letter nobody read.
> **GHOST** *(thought)*: It shows strong feelings, maybe even a motive. But someone really was poisoned tonight, so the question is not whether anger leads to murder. It is whether this letter fits one story better than the other.

### Page: Lesson 1: Think in stories

Don’t ask “who does this evidence point to?” Instead, go one person at a time and imagine they did it. Then ask: assuming that they did it, how likely is it that I would find this evidence? Imagine a hundred different possible worlds in which that person did it: on how many of them would this clue turn up? Each suspect is a story, and a clue fits some stories better than others.

### Page: Update (the player steps through, on a square of possible worlds)

- **Before**: Before the clue. The square holds every way tonight could have gone. Each person’s column is as wide as their share of my suspicion. Together they fill the whole square. *(Key: Width of a column: that person’s share of suspicion.)*
- **Step 1 of 3: shrink**: Each share shrinks by how well the clue fits that person’s story. A good fit keeps most of it; a poor fit keeps little. The shaded part is what is left; the faded part is what the clue took away. *(Key: Shaded height: how often this clue turns up in that story (your rating).)*
- **Step 2 of 3: together**: Put what’s left side by side. It no longer fills the whole square, because the clue took a piece of every share. The less that’s left, the more unusual this clue was across both stories; the more, the more ordinary. *(Key: Shaded pieces: the worlds that fit this clue. Everything else is ruled out.)*
- **Step 3 of 3: stretch**: Stretch what’s left back to the full square, keeping the proportions. Whoever kept more than the others grows; whoever kept less shrinks. This is only fair because these two stories cover every possibility between them. *(Key: New widths: the new shares. Dashed line: where the old border was.)*
- Step 1 labels each suspect “rated N: keeps very little / keeps less than half / keeps half / keeps most / keeps almost all”; step 3 marks each ▲ grew / ▼ shrank, with the old border dashed, and says in words how each share changed.

### Page: Recap: Vivid but weak evidence

An angry letter feels like a confession. But people write angry letters all the time and hurt no one. A clue only shifts suspicion as much as it fits one story better than the others. Notice how little the board moved: if your two ratings were far apart, it swung much further than a never-sent draft deserves.

*After the last clue the body glows again. The player drifts to it and chooses “Name the culprit”. In the hall, the great clock is about to strike eleven.*

Back beside my body. It is time to name the culprit.
Your board shows these beliefs. Who did it?
Look at the bars. Neither is empty. In most of the worlds that look like this one, it is [the likelier suspect]. In a few, it is [the other].
A bar is a belief, not a verdict. The living say it is better that ten guilty men go free than that one innocent man suffer. When the bars are close, I have to ask how bad it would be to accuse the wrong person, and not only who looks likelier.
Choose carefully. A ghost only gets one accusation.

> **GHOST** *(thought)*: Voss. My partner of twenty years.
> **GHOST** *(thought)*: Let me tell it the way it must have gone. I wasn’t there for it. I can only say what the evidence leaves standing.

*RECONSTRUCTION. The amber returns, but this isn’t memory: the ghost is piecing it together from what he found. Everything in this scene is imagined, and the ghost says so.*

> **GHOST** *(imagining)*: Before he left at eight, Edmund lifted the latch of the conservatory window, the one I latch myself every autumn. I never noticed.
> **GHOST** *(imagining)*: He signed my log, shook my hand, and went out smiling. He didn’t go home. He sat in his car by the garden gate, with the wet beds between him and my study window, and waited for me to make my tea.
> **GHOST** *(imagining)*: At half past nine I carried my cup to the desk and set it down. Then I left the room for a few minutes, I think. That part is still fog.
> **GHOST** *(imagining)*: Boots in the wet earth. The window rising. A hand with a silver cuff, and a little brown bottle. Then out the way he came, and one cufflink left in the fringe of the rug. Where he got the bottle, I’ll never know.
> **GHOST** *(imagining)*: And Clara, in the hall, on the telephone to Edinburgh for twenty-nine minutes, with her back to all of it.
> **GHOST** *(imagining)*: Who rang him at 7:48, I’ll never know either. It was never on the scale, and the verdict doesn’t need it. A case doesn’t have to explain every scrap.

*The amber fades. In the hall the great clock has not finished striking. Eleven. A door opens at the back of the house, and light spills down the passage from the kitchen. Footsteps. The study door.*

*CLARA enters, in a grey cardigan, a small parcel in brown paper in her hand. She sees the body on the rug and stops. The parcel falls and does not break. She kneels.*

> **CLARA**: Uncle?
> **CLARA**: ...Uncle.
> **GHOST** *(thought)*: Eleven o’clock. She always says goodnight.
> **GHOST** *(thought)*: The parcel. Brown paper and string, and a card in her handwriting: *For sleeping. I’m sorry about the house. C.* A small bottle from Hartley’s, bought at six-forty. She’s been in that kitchen for an hour, working up the courage to give it to me.
> **GHOST** *(thought)*: The clue that nearly condemned her was an olive branch. I rated it unlikely, and it was unlikely, and it still happened. Unlikely doesn’t mean never.
> **CLARA** *(whispering)*: You were right. It’s only bricks. I don’t care about the bricks. I’m sorry, Uncle. I’m so sorry.
> **GHOST** *(thought)*: It was never about the bricks, Clara. You’ll find the will in my drawer, with my note in the margin: *Talk to her.* I’d tear it in half if I had hands.
> **GHOST** *(thought)*: Tomorrow they’ll find the ledger under the blank invoices, the print behind the fern, and a man with one cufflink. Tonight there’s only this: a girl on the floor of my study, talking to me as if I could hear.
> **GHOST** *(thought)*: Perhaps I can.

*The clocks fall silent one by one. The ghost rises and fades.*

> **GHOST** *(thought)*: For the first time tonight, I can rest.

**Case closed** · You named Mr. Voss, and you were right.

## Appendix: the intended reasoning chain (designer’s notes)

**How ratings become numbers.** A rating is the likelihood P(clue | this person did it): 1 → 0.1, 2 → 0.3, 3 → 0.5, 4 → 0.7, 5 → 0.9. It never reaches 0 or 1, so no story can be ruled out, or proved, by one clue. Only the *ratio* of the two ratings moves the board, which is why a clue rated the same for both suspects changes nothing.

The board after each clue, if the player’s ratings match the intended ratings (a model answer, not an answer key):

| After | Ratings (Clara / Mr. Voss) | Likelihood ratio | Clara | Mr. Voss |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Start (household log) | — | — | 60% | 40% |
| 1. An Angry Letter | 4 / 3 | 1.4 : 1 for Clara (weak) | 68% | 32% |
| 2. The Draft Will | 5 / 5 | 1 : 1 (old news) | 68% | 32% |
| 3. A Muddy Boot Print | 1 / 3 | 5 : 1 for Voss (strong) | 30% | 70% |
| 4. The Partnership Ledger | 2 / 5 | 3 : 1 for Voss (moderate) | 12% | 88% |
| 5. The Chemist’s Slip | 4 / 2 | 2.33 : 1 for Clara (moderate) | 25% | 75% |
| 6. The Telephone Log | 1 / 3 | 5 : 1 for Voss (strong) | 6% | 94% |
| 7. A Cufflink Engraved "V" | 3 / 4 | 1.4 : 1 for Voss (weak) | 4% | 96% |

**Arc.** Clara leads, then loses ground to the print and the ledger, then comes back with the slip just when the player thinks it is settled, then is cleared by the alibi. The two clues that move the board least (the will, the cufflink) teach the lessons about old news and about clues that fit both stories. The twist and the alibi are what make the ending earn its relief.

**Timeline.** The investigation takes place between ten and eleven. Clara is in the kitchen for the whole hour and comes to the study at eleven, as she does every night. Tea at 9:30; drunk a little before ten; Clara’s call 9:28 to 9:57; Voss signed out at eight; the chemist at 6:40; Clara signed in at seven; the quarrel and the letter on Tuesday; the signing on Monday at ten and the books at eleven.

**Checks run** (by the generator, every time). Every posterior above is computed from the ratings. The final board is the same for all 5,040 orderings of the seven clues. Starting Clara at 10%, 50%, 90% and 99% (same ratings) ends at 0.3%, 3.0%, 22% and 75%: priors wash out only when everyone shares the same likelihoods. Player-error cases, for the feedback text: rating the letter 5 / 1 ends at 23% Clara; rating the will 5 / 2 (counting the quarrel twice) ends at 12%; dismissing the slip (3 / 3) ends at 2.0%; over-reacting to it (5 / 1) ends at 15%.

**Assumptions the game makes.**

1. One culprit, and the two suspects cover every possibility, so the stretch step is valid.
2. Each clue’s likelihood depends only on who did it, not on other clues found (conditional independence given the culprit). The letter and the will concern the same quarrel, so the will is deliberately rated as old news rather than as new motive evidence.
3. Background facts (Voss parks by the garden gate; Graves and Pip were out; Clara nags about John’s sleep; both appointments fall on Monday) are established before a clue uses them.
4. Verbal labels map to fixed numbers for every player. People read “likely” differently, so the labels and numbers must stay locked together.
5. The ghost searches where he already suspects, so clues are not a random sample. Likelihoods should be judged as “would I have found this here whoever did it?” The ledger reaction has the ghost admit he has not looked for evidence against Voss, and the slip is found because he then does.
6. The ghost’s unmodelled facts (the 7:48 call, where the bottle came from) stay unexplained on purpose. The verdict does not need them.
